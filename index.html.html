From Michaelis–Menten to Lineweaver–Burk

How a curved enzyme-kinetics graph becomes a straight line, and how that line reveals the difference between competitive and noncompetitive inhibitors.

1 · Derivation: take the reciprocal

The Michaelis–Menten equation gives the initial velocity v0 as a hyperbola in substrate concentration. Plotting v against [S] gives a curve that levels off, so Vmax is hard to read accurately. Taking the reciprocal of both sides straightens it out.

1

Start with Michaelis–Menten:

v = Vmax[S]Km + [S]
2

Flip both sides (take the reciprocal):

1v = Km + [S]Vmax[S]
3

Split the numerator into two fractions:

1v = KmVmax[S] + [S]Vmax[S]

Notice that [S] appears on the top and the bottom of the second fraction, so it cancels.

4

Cancel [S] in the second term and write the first as a product:

1v = KmVmax · 1[S] + 1Vmax

This matches y = mx + b with y = 1/v and x = 1/[S].

What you read off the line

  • Slope = Km / Vmax
  • y-intercept = 1 / Vmax
  • x-intercept = −1 / Km (set 1/v = 0 and solve)

3 · Competitive vs. noncompetitive

CompetitiveNoncompetitive
Where it bindsThe active site, the same place as the substrateA different site, on free enzyme or on the enzyme–substrate complex
Effect on substrate bindingSubstrate and inhibitor compete, so fewer enzymes have bound substrateSubstrate still binds, but the enzyme turns over more slowly
Overcome by more substrate?Yes, at high [S] the substrate outcompetes the inhibitorNo
VmaxUnchangedDecreases
KmIncreases (apparent affinity drops)Unchanged
Lineweaver–Burk linesCross on the y-axis (same 1/Vmax); slope increasesCross on the x-axis (same −1/Km); slope and y-intercept increase

Why Km stays the same for a noncompetitive inhibitor

Km reflects how well the enzyme binds its substrate, and only active enzyme can be measured for that. A noncompetitive inhibitor takes a fixed fraction of the enzyme out of action, but the enzyme that remains binds substrate just as well. Fewer working enzymes means a lower Vmax, not a different Km.
